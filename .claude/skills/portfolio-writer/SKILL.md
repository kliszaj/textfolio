# Portfolio Writer

## Overview

A case study is evidence of **how someone thinks**, not a record of what they
shipped. A reader who finishes it should be able to answer "why should we hire
this person?" — not merely "what has this person worked on?"

Most portfolios fail because they narrate a process everyone follows. The
interesting material is the part most people cut: the wrong assumption, the
abandoned direction, the trade-off, the pivot.

## When to Use

- Raw material exists (notes, docs, metrics, Slack threads, transcripts, links)
  and needs to become a case study entry
- An existing entry lists artefacts and phases instead of decisions
- Targeting **staff, principal, or director** — where scope and judgement are
  assessed, not screens

Not for: résumé bullets, cover letters, or a project with no outcome yet.

## The Output Contract

The entry is these parts, in this order. Every part is present; a part with no
material becomes an explicit gap, never filler.

| # | Part | What it must contain |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Positioning line** | One sentence naming the edge this project proves |
| 2 | **Problem** | What was wrong, for whom, and the evidence it was real |
| 3 | **Why the business cared** | The cost of not solving it, in their terms |
| 4 | **What I found** | Discovery, and the assumption that turned out wrong |
| 5 | **Directions explored** | What was tried and rejected — and why |
| 6 | **The turn** | The feedback or evidence that changed the thinking |
| 7 | **Trade-offs** | What was given up, and what it bought |
| 8 | **What shipped** | The solution, with craft and prototype evidence |
| 9 | **Outcome** | Measured impact — user and business |
| 10 | **What I'd do differently** | The honest retrospective |
| 11 | **Scope owned** | The surface beyond UI: product, users, business, end-to-end |

Parts 4–7 are where the case study is won. If they are thinner than parts 8–9,
the entry is still an outline.

## Workflow

1. **Intake.** Read everything supplied. Extract facts only — decisions, dates,
   numbers, quotes, constraints, names of systems. Do not begin drafting.
2. **Map to the contract.** Place each fact under a part. What remains empty is
   a gap.
3. **Interrogate the gaps.** Ask the missing questions in one batch (see below).
   Ask only what is genuinely absent; never ask what the material answers.
4. **Draft.** Fill the contract. Every claim traces to supplied material.
5. **Mark residue.** Anything still unknown ships as `[NEEDS: …]` in the draft.

## Gap Questions

Ask only the ones the material leaves open. Batch them; do not interview
one question at a time.

- What was the original problem, and how did you know it was real?
- What did you discover that you did not expect?
- Which assumption turned out to be wrong?
- What directions did you explore and reject? Why?
- What trade-offs did you make, and what did they cost?
- How did user or stakeholder feedback change your thinking?
- What was the outcome, in numbers?
- What would you do differently today?
- What did you own beyond the UI?

## The Staff-and-Above Bar

Senior evidence is a well-executed screen. Staff-and-above evidence is
judgement under ambiguity. Raise each part until it shows:

| Instead of | Show |
|---|---|
| Given a problem | Framing or reframing the problem |
| Executed the design | Chose between directions, and why |
| Worked with stakeholders | Changed a decision without authority |
| Shipped the feature | Changed how the team works, or what the product is for |
| Improved a metric | Named a metric worth moving, and defended it |

## Craft Evidence

A static image is the weakest available proof. Prefer, in order: live product,
working demo, interactive prototype, motion, then stills. If the raw material
includes a repo, deployment, or prototype link, surface it in part 8 — building
and iterating is itself the evidence.

## Selection

If several projects are supplied, do not write them all. Pick 2–3 that prove
*different* strengths (product thinking, strategy, visual craft, interaction,
systems). Say which is being cut, and why. Every entry must reinforce the same
positioning; a project that dilutes it is a project to leave out.

## Integrity

Never invent an outcome, a metric, a quote, or a user finding. A fabricated
number in a hiring artefact is a fireable discovery. Unknown material is
`[NEEDS: …]`, and the draft ships with the gaps visible.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Narrating the process everyone follows | Cut to the decisions only this person made |
| Outcome asserted, not measured | Ask for the number, or mark `[NEEDS: outcome metric]` |
| Every project claims every strength | Give each entry one job in the positioning |
| Messy parts sanded off | The dead ends *are* the differentiator — restore them |
| Interviewing one question at a time | Batch the gap questions into a single pass |
